CINEMATIC SHOWCASE / 2008

TOMB RAIDER:
UNDERWORLD.

Camera work, cinematic storytelling, and a better way to iterate.

2008CINEMATIC CAMERA WORKIN-ENGINE TOOLSWORKFLOW COLLABORATION
01 / THE WORK

Finding the shot inside the game.

On Tomb Raider: Underworld, I was responsible for the camera work on a number of in-game cinematic sequences. That meant composing shots, shaping camera movement and timing reveals around the environments and action already taking place in the game.

The three sequences below are captured from the released game. All the camera work in these clips is mine.

02 / THE PROBLEM

Every adjustment meant another round trip.

At the time, creating camera paths meant building splines in Maya, importing them into the engine, checking the result, and going back to Maya whenever something needed changing.

It was an unnecessarily cumbersome way to work, especially when the whole point of cinematic iteration is to experiment with framing, movement and timing until a sequence feels right.

03 / THE SOLUTION

Bring the camera tools into the engine.

I collaborated with an engineer to develop an in-engine camera spline system. Instead of constantly switching between Maya and the game, we could create and adjust camera paths directly in the environment where the cinematics would actually play.

That made it much easier to try different approaches, refine a shot and immediately see how it worked in context.

“If the shot lives in the game, why should I have to leave the game to adjust it?”

Other cinematic designers adopted the system later in production, and I went on to use it myself on two subsequent DLCs. What started as a practical fix to an irritating workflow became a tool with a life beyond my original sequences.
